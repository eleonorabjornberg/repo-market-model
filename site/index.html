When overnight cash runs short

Every business day, banks, dealers and money-market funds lend each other cash overnight against U.S. Treasury securities. The benchmark rate for those loans is SOFR; on 3 September 2026 it was set from about $2.9 trillion of trades. The Federal Reserve pays banks its own rate on the cash they hold with it, IORB, and on most days SOFR sits at or just below it. When spare cash runs short, SOFR climbs above.

Source repo Repo: A loan of cash against securities: one side sells a security and agrees to buy it back the next day, and the price difference is the interest. Source: cash lent until the next business day against Treasury securities. The zero line is not “normal”. It is the rate the Fed pays banks on their reserves: below it, cash in the market costs less than that, and above it, more. SOFR closed at or below zero on 1,730 of the 2,104 days counted here (82%). More than +5 bp above it, the project calls a day a pressure day.

This shows what happened, not what could have been predicted. SOFR is published at about 8:00 a.m. ET on the next business day, and may be revised at about 2:30 p.m. that day (source).

Go deeper: how this line is drawn
  • The line is SOFR minus the Fed's rate on reserves, each in percent as published, so the difference is a whole number of basis points. Before 29 July 2021 the Fed paid IOER, its rate on excess reserves, and the line uses it.
  • The +5 bp line is the project's headline pressure threshold, and a day counts only when it is strictly above it (the decision).
  • With the scale clipped at +45 bp, 4 days counted here rise above it and are drawn as triangles on the top edge; the largest was +315 bp on 17 September 2019.

The same line, with the Fed's balance-sheet runoff, the stress windows and the middle half of each day's trades, is chapter 2.

N2Why is this hard?

Of the 2,042 business days scored from 29 June 2018 to 3 September 2026, SOFR closed more than +5 bp above IORB on 145 (7%) and more than +10 bp above on 61 (3%). Most of the days above +5 bp came in 2019; the rest in 2018, 2020, 2024, 2025 and 2026.

That is what makes forecasting pressure hard. A forecast that always said “no pressure tomorrow” would have been right on 1,897 of the 2,042 days counted here, and would have missed every pressure day. So the project never grades a forecast by how often it is right: it compares it with simple benchmarks on the same days.

Pressure days also bunch together. Of the 145 days above +5 bp, 95 came on the business day after another one. One of those benchmarks simply carries today forward.

Go deeper: which days are scored
  • The marks start on 29 June 2018, not on 3 April 2018 as in N1. A forecast needs 61 days of history before it is first scored, so the first scored day is the first with that much behind it. Every day after it, to the panel's end, is scored.

N3When does it happen?

With cash scarce, SOFR closed more than +5 bp above IORB on 21 of 73 days in a quarter-end window and 121 of 978 other days counted here; with cash abundant, on 5 of 93 quarter-end window days and 1 of 960 other days.

Two things are crossed here. One is the calendar: the days around each quarter's end. The other is spare cash. Money market funds can park cash at the Fed in the Fed's overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) ON RRP: The Fed's overnight reverse repo facility: money market funds and other non-banks lend cash to the Fed overnight against its securities. Source; this view calls cash scarce on a day when the facility took in less than $100bn, and abundant otherwise.

Around quarter-ends, and to a lesser extent some month-ends, some dealers scale back repo intermediation for regulatory and accounting reporting, and the spreads they charge widen (source). Reserves and overnight reverse repo balances are cash held at the Fed by banks and by non-banks respectively (source).

The quarter-end cells are small, 73 days with scarce cash and 93 days with abundant cash, so their intervals are wide. Read each cell as a rate, not as a rule. With cash scarce, pressure also came on days outside the quarter-end window. With cash abundant, quarter-ends did not always pass quietly.

This shows what happened, not what could have been predicted.

Go deeper: how the cells are drawn
  • Quarter-end here is the column quarter_end_window: the quarter's last business day and the two business days either side, five days in all (the decision). A one-day flag on the last business day alone would put pressure on the days around it in “other days”.
  • Each day is classed by the ON RRP result public at 4 pm New York time that day. The project dates a result to 16:00 on the next business day, because the New York Fed states no publication time, so the reading is the previous business day's operation. The amount is what the facility accepted that day, summed over its operations, from the New York Fed's operation results (source).
  • The $100bn break is the project's fixed constant, taken from the advisor evidence pack and not tuned by this project on these days. The pack drew it from the same history, so it was not chosen blind to them: the review of that pack (#96) re-checked it on our definitions, on days before 2026 only. The reserve-scarcity state in the band above counts the same break, but reads it with that week's bank assets, so its reading can be older than this one.
  • A day counts when its spread, in whole basis points, is strictly above the line (the decision). Each interval is a 90% stationary-bootstrap interval that resamples a cell's days in blocks averaging 10 days, because pressure days come in runs.

The band:

  • The state adds two readings. Reserves over the total assets of all commercial banks: 0 at or above 13%, 1 from 12% up to it, 2 below 12%. New York Fed research measures reserves against total bank assets, and puts the point where reserves stop being plentiful at about 12–13% of bank assets (source). And the overnight reverse repo balance: 0 at or above $100bn, 1 below it. The sum runs from 0 to 3: 0 abundant; 1 ample; 2 tight; 3 scarce.
  • Each day shows the state as it could be read at 4 pm New York time that day, from the latest week all its inputs were public. The Federal Reserve Board publishes commercial banks' weekly balance sheets, total assets among them, in its H.8 release, generally on Fridays at 4:15 p.m. Eastern Time (source). Bank assets are each week's first print. Reserves are the latest revised figures, so that leg is shown with hindsight.
  • The bottom lane marks the days the cells above call scarce cash, from the same 4 pm reading. Each interval is a 90% stationary-bootstrap interval that resamples a state's days in blocks averaging 10 days, the one #115's validation used.

N4Who lends to whom

Of the 18 public series on this market's map, six are read by a published forecast. Each tag on the map says which, and why or why not; it is worked out from the repository, not typed.

Cash moves between the Fed, the Treasury, money funds, banks, dealers, the funds dealers lend to, and other cash lenders such as the Federal Home Loan Banks. Each public series that measures one of those flows is a tag: the balance at the Fed's overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) ON RRP: The Fed's overnight reverse repo facility: money market funds and other non-banks lend cash to the Fed overnight against its securities. Source, the balance of the Treasury General Account (TGA) TGA: The U.S. Treasury's own account at the Fed. Payments into it, such as taxes, move cash out of bank accounts and drain reserves. Source, the gap between EFFR EFFR: The effective federal funds rate: the New York Fed's measure of what banks pay to borrow reserves overnight without collateral. Source and IORB, take-up at the Fed's standing repo operations (SRF) Standing repo (SRF): The Fed lends cash overnight in repo to eligible dealers and banks, at a rate it sets; often called the Standing Repo Facility. Source, and the percentile Percentile: The rate below which a given share of the day's trading volume was struck: the 99th percentile is near the top of the day's rates. Sources of each day's SOFR trades, among others. Commercial banks and Federal Home Loan Banks are the main participants in the federal funds market, and the Home Loan Banks make up the vast majority of its lending (source).

Three of the New York Fed's rates price the repo segments. TGCR is tri-party repo Tri-party repo: Repo settled through a clearing bank that holds the collateral for both sides; the New York Fed's TGCR measures its rate. Source general collateral repo, leaving out the Fed's own trades. BGCR is tri-party plus GCF Repo GCF Repo: FICC's general collateral finance repo service; the New York Fed's BGCR adds its trades to tri-party repo. Source. SOFR is BGCR plus FICC-cleared DVP repo DVP repo: Delivery-versus-payment repo cleared through FICC, in which each trade names the security it settles. Source, with the lowest-rate 20 percent of DVP volume trimmed. Non-centrally cleared bilateral repo, traded directly between two firms without a clearing house, is in none of the three. The three rates are built only from tri-party, GCF Repo and FICC-cleared DVP trades, so non-centrally cleared bilateral repo is in none of them (source).

The result

The final test

From 2 January to 3 September 2026, the model's next-day forecast of the range of SOFR − IORB was more accurate than carrying the latest spread forward: 1.66 bp against 1.83 bp of five-quantile score, where lower is better, and the 90% interval of the gap, +0.03 to +0.36 bp, lies above zero. Result: pass, on a test that is near-blind, not blind: these days had appeared inside earlier pooled results, though the record says no choice was made on them by name.

The claim, as pre-registered: the published model's one-day-ahead forecast of the five published quantiles of SOFR − IORB was more accurate than as-of persistence over January to September 2026. It is a pass on a near-blind test, and a statement about the range forecast, not a warning of stress.

How the test was run

  • Fixed first. The design, the benchmark and the command were written down and checksummed (d0847824027e) before any of these days was scored (the pre-registration).
  • Run once, on 5 October 2026, on Eleonora's go, and published whatever it showed.
  • Against carrying the latest spread forward. The benchmark, as-of persistence, forecasts the next day from the latest spread known at the decision time. Both are graded by the five-quantile score (the mean pinball loss over the five published quantiles): how far a forecast range was from the spread that actually came, in basis points, so lower is better.
  • The deciding comparison was changed before the test was opened. On 4 October 2026 the deciding cell was changed from the plain-leap probability cell (#216) to this five-quantile score cell, under Eleonora's ruling on #221, recorded in the pre-registration's amendment.

What it does not show

  • What the pass rests on, post hoc. Two days carry 55.6% of the summed paired difference: 5 January 2026 and 6 January 2026 (+11.22 bp and +5.13 bp). Without them the mean paired difference is +0.078 bp, 90% interval −0.014 to +0.180 bp, by the record's own bootstrap (mean block length 2, seed 1970125677, 2,000 replications), which does not separate it from zero. The model beat persistence on 87 of 169 days. On this near-blind test, this was computed after the result: it decides nothing, and the verdict stands.
  • It is not a warning of stress. It grades the forecast range, not the chance of pressure. One day ahead, the event cells for days more than +5 bp and +10 bp above IORB are inconclusive, with 5 and 0 such days in the window. Below 20 such days, a cell is labelled inconclusive.
  • It is near-blind, not blind. These days had appeared inside earlier pooled results, though the record says no choice was made on them by name. A blind test waits on the days from 4 September 2026 on: the blind tier, which no test has opened.
  • 2026 was calm, and was known to be calm when the test was designed; every scored day falls in one regime, 2025–26. The test says nothing about a stressed period.
  • The five-quantile score two to five days ahead is not evidence. Those cells use a different model from the one-day forecast, and each carries Eleonora's label.

2Eight years in one line

SOFR closed above the Fed's rate on reserves on 1 day in 2021–23, and on 111 days in 2025–26. It was more than 50 bp above on 4 days, all in 2018–19.

  2. 17 Sep 2019 SOFR prints 5.25%, 315 bp above IOER. Source
  3. 11 Oct 2019 Fed announces Treasury bill purchases to keep reserves ample. Source
  4. 15 Mar 2020 Fed cuts rates to near zero and announces large asset purchases. Source

Go deeper: how this chart is built
  • SOFR is the volume-weighted median rate of overnight repo trades backed by Treasury securities. SOFR is published at about 8:00 a.m. ET on the next business day, and may be revised at about 2:30 p.m. that day (source).
  • The Fed's rate on reserves is IORB from 29 July 2021. Before that the Fed paid IOER, the rate on excess reserves, and the chart uses it so that September 2019 and March 2020 are covered. The switch is marked as event 5.
  • The middle half of the day's trades is the band between SOFR's 25th and 75th volume-weighted percentiles, less the same rate. The New York Fed did not publish it on 2 days in the sample.
  • +5 and +10 bp are the project's headline thresholds: the forecast gives the probability that tomorrow's spread is above each of them.
  • The stress windows, 16–20 September 2019 and 9–20 March 2020, are frozen and checksummed. Every published result pools those days with the rest.
  • In the full view the scale stops at +45 bp so that ordinary days stay readable. 4 days rise above it and leave the frame; the largest, +315 bp on 17 September 2019, is labelled, and hovering shows the rest.

3Why it happens

On pressure days in 2018–19, reserves had a median of $1.52 trillion. In 2025–26 the median was $2.93 trillion.

Banks need more reserves as their balance sheets grow and as rules tighten, so a dollar figure from one period does not compare with one from another. New York Fed research measures reserves against total bank assets, and puts the point where reserves stop being plentiful at about 12–13% of bank assets (source). The project will use that ratio once the bank-assets series is added.

This chart describes what happened together. It does not show that one caused the other.

In 2018–19, pressure came on 65 of 365 (18%) ordinary days. In 2025–26 it came on 21 of 352 (6%), but on 2 of 6 quarter-ends. In 2021–23, when reserves were most plentiful, there were none.

The usual explanations are these, and the table does not test them. Around quarter-ends, and to a lesser extent some month-ends, some dealers scale back repo intermediation for regulatory and accounting reporting, and the spreads they charge widen (source). On corporate tax dates, payments move from bank and money-fund accounts into the Treasury's account at the Fed, which drains reserves (source). When Treasury auctions settle, primary dealers pay for the securities they bought and finance them in repo, so more Treasuries need financing with the same cash (source). Dealers and hedge funds borrow in repo to finance coupon Treasuries, so days when new coupon securities are issued raise the demand for repo cash (source).

Go deeper: how the days are classified

Each day is counted once, in this order:

  • Quarter-end: the last business day of March, June, September or December (the panel's quarter_end flag).
  • Month-end: the last business day of any other month.
  • Tax window: the quarterly tax deadlines and the business days after each that the panel's tax_date flag marks.
  • Coupon settlement: a day on which Treasury notes or bonds sold at auction settle.
  • Other: every remaining business day, bill settlements included.

A type with few days a year, such as quarter-ends, moves a long way on a single day. Read the counts, not only the shade. The periods are the regimes of metadata/evaluation_splits.json, the period slices declared before the reserve-scarcity state existed; re-declaring them by that state is a decision still open.

4What's known at 4 pm

Each forecast is made at 4 pm the business day before. By then the Treasury settlements for the next day are already announced; of the observed inputs, the freshest is 1 day old and the stalest is dealer Treasury positions, 7 business days old.

Go deeper: what this does and does not show
  • Each dot is drawn from the declaration the as-of rule itself reads (the information rule), through the same function. A lag counted in business days skips weekends and holidays; one counted in calendar days does not, so its dot can fall on a day with no decision.
  • The weekly H.4.1 series, reserves and the TGA, are declared five calendar days after the row's date, so each is read 5 to 12 days after its Thursday print. A newer print was public and unread on about two scored days in five. That is the safe direction, not a leak.
  • It shows the clock only. How much a stale input costs a forecast is a model result and appears only in the model chapters.

5Making a forecast

At +5 bp, three of the four scored models beat calendar-type climatology with a 90% interval above zero, and one beats the persistence-logistic. At +10 bp, one beats calendar-type climatology, and none beats the persistence-logistic.

Go deeper: how the forecasts are scored
  • Each model gives, at 4 pm on the business day before, the probability that the next day's spread is above +5 bp and above +10 bp. It reads only what was public at that instant (the information rule).
  • The Brier score is the mean squared gap between the stated probability and what happened, 1 or 0. Lower is better.
  • The benchmarks are those the project fixed in advance: a climatology by type of day, and a logistic model of the latest spread (the pressure-probability rule). Each is scored in the same run as the model and paired with it day by day.
  • The intervals are stationary-bootstrap intervals on the daily differences, because neighbouring days are not independent draws.
  • This chapter shows four scored models. Which of them, if any, becomes the published pressure probability is decided in a scored record under that rule, not on this page.

6Grading it honestly

At +5 bp, pressure came on 7.5% of the scored days. The share of a model's Brier score that is miscalibration runs from 5% (climatology) to 16% (gradient-boosted distribution, cross-conformal, 4 inputs).

Lead time

No published record in docs/runs/ carries lead time to the onset of pressure, so this page draws no figure of it. The generator draws model figures only from such a record, and refuses one that no record carries.

Go deeper: reading the reliability curve
  • CORP sorts the forecasts and fits the best non-decreasing curve through what happened. Where the curve sits below the diagonal, the model said pressure was likelier than it turned out; above it, less likely.
  • The Brier score splits exactly into three parts: miscalibration, the curve's distance from the diagonal; discrimination, how far the recalibrated forecasts move away from the base rate; and uncertainty, which depends only on how often pressure came. A good forecast has little of the first and much of the second.
  • Most days carry a forecast near zero, so the curve is drawn on the range the forecasts actually used.

7How it was built

Every figure on this page is computed from a verified data panel by a script in the repository, and every change to that script went through a written directive and a reviewed pull request.

  1. DirectiveWork starts from a directive: an issue with a goal, acceptance criteria and the files to read. Rule
  2. SessionOne session works one directive on its own branch, test first. Rule
  3. Pull requestThe pull request is the report: what changed, the evidence, what was checked and what was not. Rule
  4. ReviewA separate reviewer checks every acceptance criterion against the tree, and judgement calls go to Eleonora. Rule
  5. PublishA record is published only while nothing that computes a figure, declares a source or supplies the panel has changed since it was scored. Rule

The guards

  • The as-of rule. Each forecast reads every input at its latest value public at the decision time the day before, field by field. Source
  • Leakage and staleness guards. A read newer than the decision instant raises LookAheadError; a read older than the latest admissible value raises StaleReadError. Source
  • The panel digest. The panel rebuilds from tracked files and is refused unless its SHA-256 matches the published manifest. Source
  • A locked test period. Recent days are held back from every model comparison until a phase verdict opens them. Source

A validation result

An audit of the scoring code found that every published record had read its inputs later than they were public. That leaks nothing, but it is not what a forecaster at the decision time would have had, so every record was archived and re-scored under the as-of rule (source).

Terms used on this page
Repo
A loan of cash against securities, here overnight and against Treasuries. The borrower sells the securities and agrees to buy them back the next day at a slightly higher price.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of a percentage point.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate: the typical rate on overnight Treasury repo, published by the New York Fed.
TGCR, BGCR
The New York Fed's rates for the tri-party segment alone, and for tri-party plus GCF Repo.
IORB, IOER
The interest the Fed pays banks on their reserve balances; IOER until 28 July 2021, IORB since 29 July 2021.
Reserves
Cash that banks hold in their accounts at the Fed. It is the system's most liquid cash.
Overnight reverse repo
The Fed borrows cash overnight, mostly from money market funds, against its securities.
Standing repo
The Fed lends cash overnight against Treasuries at a rate it sets; often called the Standing Repo Facility.
Balance-sheet runoff
The Fed letting its securities mature without replacing them, which removes cash from the financial system. Often called quantitative tightening.
Settlement
The day a Treasury sold at auction is paid for and delivered.
