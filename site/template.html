When overnight cash runs short

Every business day, banks, dealers and money-market funds lend each other cash overnight against U.S. Treasury securities. The benchmark rate for those loans is SOFR; on {{shown_day}} it was set from about {{latest_volume}} of trades. The Federal Reserve pays banks its own rate on the cash they hold with it, IORB, and on most days SOFR sits at or just below it. When spare cash runs short, SOFR climbs above.

SOFR is the typical rate on {{dfn_overnight}} {{dfn_repo}}: cash lent until the next business day against Treasury securities. Much of that cash comes from {{dfn_money_fund}} and goes to {{dfn_dealer}} (source).

The zero line is not “normal”. It is the rate the Fed pays banks on their reserves: below it, cash in the market costs less than that, and above it, more. SOFR closed at or below zero on {{n1_at_or_below}}. More than +{{pressure_bp}} bp above it, the project calls a day a pressure day.

This shows what happened, not what could have been predicted. {{c_sofr_publication}}

Go deeper: how this line is drawn
  • The line is SOFR minus the Fed's rate on reserves, each in percent as published, so the difference is a whole number of basis points. Before {{n1_iorb_from}} the Fed paid IOER, its rate on excess reserves, and the line uses it.
  • The +{{pressure_bp}} bp line is the project's headline pressure threshold, and a day counts only when it is strictly above it (the decision).
  • With the scale clipped at +{{clip_bp}} bp, {{n1_off_scale}} counted here rise above it and are drawn as triangles on the top edge; the largest was {{n1_spike}}.

The same line, with the Fed's balance-sheet runoff, the stress windows and the middle half of each day's trades, is chapter 2.

N4Who lends to whom

{{n4_lede}}

Cash moves between the Fed, the Treasury, money funds, banks, dealers, the funds dealers lend to, and other cash lenders such as the Federal Home Loan Banks. Each public series that measures one of those flows is a tag: the balance at {{dfn_on_rrp}}, the balance of {{dfn_tga}}, the gap between {{dfn_effr}} and IORB, take-up at {{dfn_srf}}, and the {{dfn_percentile}}s of each day's SOFR trades, among others. {{c_fhlb_fed_funds}}

Three of the New York Fed's rates price the repo segments. TGCR is {{dfn_triparty}} general collateral repo, leaving out the Fed's own trades. BGCR is tri-party plus {{dfn_gcf}}. SOFR is BGCR plus FICC-cleared {{dfn_dvp}}, with the lowest-rate 20 percent of DVP volume trimmed. Non-centrally cleared bilateral repo, traded directly between two firms without a clearing house, is in none of the three. {{c_segments_none}}

Go deeper: the status of every tag, and the segment days {{n4_status_table}}

Go deeper: how this chart is built
  • SOFR is the volume-weighted median rate of overnight repo trades backed by Treasury securities. {{c_sofr_publication}}
  • The Fed's rate on reserves is IORB from {{iorb_from}}. Before that the Fed paid IOER, the rate on excess reserves, and the chart uses it so that {{holdout_months}} are covered. The switch is marked as event {{iorb_event_n}}.
  • The middle half of the day's trades is the band between SOFR's 25th and 75th volume-weighted percentiles, less the same rate. The New York Fed did not publish it on {{band_missing}} in the sample.
  • +{{pressure_bp}} and +{{second_bp}} bp are the project's headline thresholds: the forecast gives the probability that tomorrow's spread is above each of them.
  • The stress windows, {{holdouts}}, are frozen and checksummed. Every published result pools those days with the rest.
  • In the full view the scale stops at +{{clip_bp}} bp so that ordinary days stay readable. {{n_off_scale}} rise above it and leave the frame; the largest, {{spike_bp}} bp on {{spike_day}}, is labelled, and hovering shows the rest.

3Why it happens

On pressure days in {{early}}, reserves had a median of {{med_early}}. In {{late}} the median was {{med_late}}.

Banks need more reserves as their balance sheets grow and as rules tighten, so a dollar figure from one period does not compare with one from another. {{c_reserve_ratio}} The project will use that ratio once the bank-assets series is added.

This chart describes what happened together. It does not show that one caused the other.

In {{early}}, pressure came on {{ord_early}} ordinary days. In {{late}} it came on {{ord_late}}, but on {{qe_late}} quarter-ends. In {{ample}}, when reserves were most plentiful, {{quiet_ample}}.

The usual explanations are these, and the table does not test them. {{c_quarter_end}} {{c_tax_date}} {{c_settlement}} {{c_coupon_demand}}

Go deeper: how the days are classified

Each day is counted once, in this order:

  • Quarter-end: the last business day of March, June, September or December (the panel's quarter_end flag).
  • Month-end: the last business day of any other month.
  • Tax window: the quarterly tax deadlines and the business days after each that the panel's tax_date flag marks.
  • Coupon settlement: a day on which Treasury notes or bonds sold at auction settle.
  • Other: every remaining business day, bill settlements included.

A type with few days a year, such as quarter-ends, moves a long way on a single day. Read the counts, not only the shade. The periods are the regimes of metadata/evaluation_splits.json, the period slices declared before the reserve-scarcity state existed; re-declaring them by that state is a decision still open.

4What's known at {{decision}}

Each forecast is made at {{decision}} the business day before. By then the {{sched_names}} for the next day are already announced; of the observed inputs, the freshest is {{lag_min}} and the stalest is {{lag_max}}.

Go deeper: what this does and does not show
  • Each dot is drawn from the declaration the as-of rule itself reads (the information rule), through the same function. A lag counted in business days skips weekends and holidays; one counted in calendar days does not, so its dot can fall on a day with no decision.
  • The weekly H.4.1 series, reserves and the TGA, are declared five calendar days after the row's date, so each is read 5 to 12 days after its Thursday print. A newer print was public and unread on about two scored days in five. That is the safe direction, not a leak.
  • It shows the clock only. How much a stale input costs a forecast is a model result and appears only in the model chapters.

To see which value each input supplies on a given day, run section 5 of the overview notebook in Colab.

5Making a forecast

{{forecast_lede}}

Go deeper: how the forecasts are scored
  • Each model gives, at {{decision}} on the business day before, the probability that the next day's spread is above +{{tau_first}} bp and above +{{tau_second}} bp. It reads only what was public at that instant (the information rule).
  • The Brier score is the mean squared gap between the stated probability and what happened, 1 or 0. Lower is better.
  • The benchmarks are those the project fixed in advance: a climatology by type of day, and a logistic model of the latest spread (the pressure-probability rule). Each is scored in the same run as the model and paired with it day by day.
  • The intervals are stationary-bootstrap intervals on the daily differences, because neighbouring days are not independent draws.
  • This chapter shows {{n_scored_models}} scored models. Which of them, if any, becomes the published pressure probability is decided in a scored record under that rule, not on this page.

Go deeper: reading the reliability curve
  • CORP sorts the forecasts and fits the best non-decreasing curve through what happened. Where the curve sits below the diagonal, the model said pressure was likelier than it turned out; above it, less likely.
  • The Brier score splits exactly into three parts: miscalibration, the curve's distance from the diagonal; discrimination, how far the recalibrated forecasts move away from the base rate; and uncertainty, which depends only on how often pressure came. A good forecast has little of the first and much of the second.
  • Most days carry a forecast near zero, so the curve is drawn on the range the forecasts actually used.

Terms used on this page
Repo
A loan of cash against securities, here overnight and against Treasuries. The borrower sells the securities and agrees to buy them back the next day at a slightly higher price.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of a percentage point.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate: the typical rate on overnight Treasury repo, published by the New York Fed.
TGCR, BGCR
The New York Fed's rates for the tri-party segment alone, and for tri-party plus GCF Repo.
IORB, IOER
The interest the Fed pays banks on their reserve balances; IOER until {{ioer_until}}, IORB since {{iorb_from}}.
Reserves
Cash that banks hold in their accounts at the Fed. It is the system's most liquid cash.
Overnight reverse repo
The Fed borrows cash overnight, mostly from money market funds, against its securities.
Standing repo
The Fed lends cash overnight against Treasuries at a rate it sets; often called the Standing Repo Facility.
Balance-sheet runoff
The Fed letting its securities mature without replacing them, which removes cash from the financial system. Often called quantitative tightening.
Settlement
The day a Treasury sold at auction is paid for and delivered.
