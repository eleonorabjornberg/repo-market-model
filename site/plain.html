The question

Banks, dealers and money funds lend each other cash overnight against U.S. Treasury securities. This is the repo market. The rate on that lending is called SOFR. The Federal Reserve also pays banks a rate on the money they keep with it, called IORB. Most days SOFR sits at or just below IORB. On some days spare cash runs short and SOFR jumps above it.

This project forecasts the gap between the two, SOFR minus IORB, for the next business day. The headline forecast is the chance that the gap is more than 5 and more than 10 basis points. A basis point is a hundredth of one percentage point. It uses public data only, and only what was public at 4 pm on the day before the forecast. The public data runs from 2018-04-03 to 2026-09-03.

Where this comes from: metadata/stress_thresholds.json, metadata/funding_panel_manifest.json, and the validation report.

How often it is right about pressure

Each day the model gives a probability that the gap will be above the line. A good probability forecast is neither too bold nor too timid. The usual measure is the Brier score: how far the forecast probability was from what happened, on average, where lower is better. Each result below is a paired difference: on every day, the benchmark's score minus the model's, averaged. A positive number means the model did better. The range after it is the 90% interval, found by resampling the days.

Two benchmarks keep the model honest. The calendar guess uses only how often pressure happens on each type of day (month-end, quarter-end, tax date, ordinary). The “tomorrow looks like today” model looks at today's gap and nothing else.

  • More than +5 bp. This happened on 7.5% of the 1873 days scored (every day up to 2025-12-31). against the calendar guess the model was better (+0.0219, 90% interval +0.0158 to +0.0282); against the “tomorrow looks like today” model the model was better (+0.0068, 90% interval +0.0031 to +0.0107).
  • More than +10 bp. This happened on 3.3% of the 1873 days scored (every day up to 2025-12-31). against the calendar guess the model was better (+0.0039, 90% interval +0.0015 to +0.0065); against the “tomorrow looks like today” model the model was not clearly different (+0.0007, 90% interval -0.0010 to +0.0024).

The gain is not the same everywhere. Against the “tomorrow looks like today” model at +5 bp, the model was better in 6 of 9 groups of days, not clearly different in 2, worse in 1, and 0 had too few days to say.

The forecast range for tomorrow

The model also forecasts the whole range of where the gap could land, not just one number. This is the five-quantile score: it measures how far off the forecast range was, and lower is better. The benchmark is as-of persistence, which says tomorrow will look like the latest gap already public.

  • Earlier years. From 2018-06-29 to 2025-12-31 (1873 forecasts) the model's score was 1.66 bp and persistence's was 2.09 bp. The gap is +0.43 bp, 90% interval +0.19 to +0.74 bp, so the model was better.
  • The final test, January to September 2026. Over 169 scored days the model's score was 1.66 bp and persistence's was 1.83 bp. The gap's 90% interval, +0.03 to +0.36 bp, lies above zero, so the model was better. A few days drive the mean, and 2026 was a calm year.

The final test was set up before it was run and run once, on days the model had not been tuned on by name. Those days had appeared inside earlier pooled results, so it is called near-blind, not blind. The first fully blind confirmation will come from the live daily record.

How scarce cash is

Pressure is more likely when bank reserves, the cash banks keep at the Fed, are scarce. The model therefore shows a scarcity state beside each forecast: one of abundant, ample, tight, scarce. It is a label for what is happening now. It is not an input to the forecast, and it is not itself a forecast.

The label is set by two cut-points. Reserves count as ample or tight depending on where they stand as a share of all commercial-bank assets: 12% to 13% is the band where banks stop treating reserves as abundant. And the Fed's overnight reverse repo facility is a buffer only while it holds more than $100 billion; below that it no longer absorbs a drain on reserves. Both cut-points were chosen from the literature and from the same history they are checked on, so they describe the market and have not been proven in a separate test.

Besides the pressure probability and the scarcity state, the model's daily outputs are the range of the gap on scheduled pressure days one to five days ahead, and the expected contribution of a month-end or quarter-end turn to the period average. They report on the same frozen model. They are described in the validation report and have no separate scored record yet.

Where this comes from: src/repo_model/scarcity.py, scripts/desk_outputs.py, the validation report.

Where it falls short

This is a research forecast of next-day overnight borrowing costs, not a warning system or a tool for managing risk. It has been tested mostly in calm conditions, so it needs re-checking if the market changes sharply.

  • Few severe days. Of the 169 days of the final test, 5 were above +5 bp and 0 above +10 bp. Such days are rare, so the evidence about them is thin.
  • The test above +10 bp is weak. On the earlier years the model's gain over the “tomorrow looks like today” model at +10 bp has a 90% interval of -0.0010 to +0.0024, so it was not clearly different. Against the calendar guess it was better.
  • Days that are locked. Some days are kept sealed so that no result can be tuned on them. A comparison scores only days before 2026-01-01. The older pooled distribution tables scored days through 2026-09-03 (the near-blind period); the results published since score only days before 2026-01-01.
The statement of record

A research forecast of the SOFR − IORB spread. It is not a stress-warning system and not a basis for VaR, limits, liquidity or capital, or desk sizing. Its central quantiles are not yet calibrated, its tail coverage is weaker on quarter-ends and turns, and it has little history in the current scarce-reserve regime. It needs revalidation after a material policy or regime change.

Where this sits among other work

To our knowledge, no public work forecasts the daily SOFR − IORB spread with strictly point-in-time public data and scores its exceedance probabilities with proper scoring rules against persistence baselines. Related efforts include Fed staff probability assessments for the fed funds − IOER spread (FOMC memo, 13 Sep 2019), a daily SOFR LSTM thesis scored by RMSE (Cornelissen, 2021), and futures-implied month-end premia, which tend to over-anticipate spikes (Gellert & Schlögl, 2021).

  • FOMC memo, 13 September 2019 (Fed staff, the fed funds − IOER spread)
  • Cornelissen (2021), the daily SOFR LSTM thesis
  • Gellert and Schlögl (2021), futures-implied month-end premia

nicholasberoud/repo-market-model is a fork by Eleonora's collaborator, Nicholas Beroud. A reader who finds that repository with the same figures has found a copy of this project, not an independent replication.

Futures are left out, both as a benchmark and as an input. CME futures data needs a licence and cannot be redistributed with this repository, and futures-implied month-end premia are documented to over-anticipate spikes (Gellert and Schlögl, 2021; CME's March 2019 example).
