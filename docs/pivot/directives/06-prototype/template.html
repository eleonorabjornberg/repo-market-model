When overnight cash runs short

Every business day, banks, dealers and money-market funds lend each other cash overnight against U.S. Treasury securities. The benchmark rate for those loans is SOFR; on {{last_day}} it was set from about {{latest_volume}} of trades. The Federal Reserve pays banks its own rate on the cash they hold with it, IORB, and on most days SOFR sits at or just below it. When spare cash runs short, SOFR climbs above.

Go deeper: how this chart is built
  • SOFR is published by the New York Fed each morning for the previous business day. It is the volume-weighted median rate of overnight repo trades backed by Treasury securities.
  • The Fed's rate on reserves is IORB from 29 July 2021. Before that the Fed paid IOER, the rate on excess reserves, and the chart uses it so that September 2019 and March 2020 are covered. The switch is marked as event 5.
  • The middle half of the day's trades is the band between SOFR's 25th and 75th volume-weighted percentiles, less the same rate. The New York Fed did not publish it on {{band_missing}} in the sample.
  • +5 and +10 bp are the project's headline thresholds: the forecast will give the probability that tomorrow's spread reaches each of them.
  • The stress windows, {{holdouts}}, are frozen and checksummed. They are kept out of the headline score and reported on their own, so that two extreme weeks cannot dominate the average.
  • In the full view the scale stops at +{{clip_bp}} bp so that ordinary days stay readable. {{n_off_scale}} rise above it and leave the frame; the largest is labelled, and hovering shows the rest.

3Why it happens

On pressure days in 2018–19, reserves had a median of {{med_1819}}. In 2025–26 the median was {{med_2526}}.

Banks need more reserves as their balance sheets grow and as rules tighten, so a dollar figure from 2019 does not compare with one from 2025. New York Fed research measures reserves against total bank assets instead, and puts the point where reserves stop being plentiful at about 12–13% of bank assets (Afonso, Giannone, La Spada and Williams). The project will use that ratio once the bank-assets series is added.

This chart describes what happened together. It does not show that one caused the other.

In 2019, pressure came on {{ord_2019}} ordinary days. In 2025 it came on {{ord_2025}}, but on {{qe_2025}} quarter-ends. In 2021–23, with reserves abundant, {{quiet_2021_23}}.

The usual explanations are these. At quarter-ends, some banks, notably foreign banks that report balance sheets as of that day, lend less in repo. Month-ends are similar but milder, and many Treasury coupon securities settle then. On tax deadlines, payments move cash from bank deposits into the Treasury's account at the Fed, which drains reserves. On settlement days, buyers of newly issued Treasuries have to pay for them, and dealers finance the securities they keep in repo.

Go deeper: how the days are classified

Each day is counted once, in this order:

  • Quarter-end: the last business day of March, June, September or December.
  • Month-end: the last business day of any other month.
  • Tax window: the quarterly tax deadlines in April, June, September and December, and the two business days after each (the panel's tax_date flag).
  • Coupon settlement: a day on which Treasury notes or bonds sold at auction settle.
  • Other: every remaining business day, bill settlements included.

Quarter-ends number four a year, so a single day moves a cell by 25 points. Read the counts, not only the shade.

Terms used on this page
Repo
A loan of cash against securities, here overnight and against Treasuries. The borrower sells the securities and agrees to buy them back the next day at a slightly higher price.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of a percentage point. A move from 4.30% to 4.35% is 5 bp.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate: the typical rate on overnight Treasury repo, published by the New York Fed.
IORB, IOER
The interest the Fed pays banks on their reserve balances; IOER until 28 July 2021, IORB since.
Reserves
Cash that banks hold in their accounts at the Fed. It is the system's most liquid cash.
Balance-sheet runoff
The Fed letting its securities mature without replacing them, which removes cash from the financial system. Often called quantitative tightening.
Settlement
The day a Treasury sold at auction is paid for and delivered.
